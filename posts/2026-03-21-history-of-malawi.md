Malawi is one of sub-Saharan Africa's most densely populated countries, a landlocked nation shaped by ancient kingdoms, the violence of the slave trade, Scottish missionary ambition, and a long struggle for self-determination. Understanding this history is essential context for anyone traveling there — including our team heading to Ekwendeni this April.

---

## Part I: Pre-Colonization

### Early Inhabitants

The earliest known inhabitants of the Lake Malawi basin were the San people, known locally as the *Akafula* — small-statured hunter-gatherers who left rock art across the region. Beginning in the first through fourth centuries CE, Bantu-speaking agriculturalists arrived in successive migrations from central and west Africa, gradually displacing or absorbing earlier populations.

A second, larger Bantu migration between the 13th and 15th centuries brought the ancestors of the Chewa, Tumbuka, Tonga, and other groups who still form the core of Malawi's population today.

### The Maravi Confederacy (~1480–early 1700s)

The most powerful political structure in the region was the **Maravi Confederacy**, founded by the Chewa people (also called the Amaravi) who migrated from the area of present-day Democratic Republic of Congo. By the late 15th century, the Maravi had established a confederacy stretching across what is now Malawi, eastern Zambia, and northern Mozambique.

The confederacy was led by a paramount chief known as the **Kalonga**, whose court was near the southern end of Lake Malawi. At its height, the Maravi controlled long-distance trade in ivory, iron, and cloth across a vast swath of southeastern Africa. The name "Malawi" itself is thought to derive from *Maravi* — meaning "flames" in the Chewa language, possibly referring to the glinting surface of the lake.

### Other Major Groups

The **Tumbuka** people settled in the northern highlands — the region around Ekwendeni and Mzimba — and developed their own chieftaincies and cultural traditions. The Tumbuka language remains the dominant language of northern Malawi today.

To the south and east, **Yao** communities established themselves as key nodes in Indian Ocean trade networks, converting to Islam through contact with Arab and Swahili merchants. The Yao would later become central intermediaries in the devastating 19th-century slave trade.

In the 1820s–1840s, **Ngoni** groups arrived from southern Africa, fleeing the upheaval known as the *Mfecane* — a period of widespread warfare and displacement set off by the rise of the Zulu Kingdom. Skilled warriors, the Ngoni raided and absorbed many Tumbuka communities in the north, leaving a deep mark on the region's identity.

### The Arab-Swahili Slave Trade

From the 18th century onward, Arab and Swahili traders pushed inland from the East African coast, establishing slave-raiding networks that reached deep into the lake region. The Yao became major intermediaries, raiding Chewa and Tumbuka villages and marching captives to the coast — primarily to Kilwa Kivinje on the Tanzanian coast — where they were shipped to Zanzibar, the Middle East, and the Americas.

By the mid-19th century, the trade had devastated entire regions around Lake Malawi. It was the abolitionist outcry this trade generated — particularly through the writings of David Livingstone — that brought European attention to the area.

## Part II: Colonization (1859–1964)

### Livingstone and the Missionaries

**David Livingstone** arrived at Lake Malawi on September 17, 1859, becoming the first European to reach it. He named it "Lake Nyasa" (*nyasa* simply meaning "lake" in Yao). His dispatches back to Britain — documenting the scale of the slave trade and the suffering he witnessed — galvanized the Scottish church establishment.

Two major mission stations followed:
- **Livingstonia Mission** (1875), established by the Free Church of Scotland in the north
- **Blantyre Mission** (1876), established by the Church of Scotland in the south

**Ekwendeni itself is a mission-era town.** The Livingstonia Mission extended its reach northward, and Ekwendeni became a Church of Scotland outpost in the Mzimba District. Ekwendeni Mission Hospital — where our team will be working — is a direct institutional descendant of that 19th-century mission presence.

### The British Protectorate

Partly to suppress the slave trade and partly to counter Portuguese and German encroachment, Britain declared the **British Central Africa Protectorate** in 1889, ratified in 1891. **Harry Johnston** was appointed the first Commissioner, and he moved aggressively to dismantle the slave trade, negotiating with — and often militarily defeating — the Yao chiefs who controlled it.

In 1907 the territory was renamed **Nyasaland**. The colonial economy was built on cotton and tobacco estates, many owned by Scottish settlers. African laborers were required to work for reduced wages or in lieu of hut taxes — a system of economic coercion that generated deep and lasting resentment.

### John Chilembwe and the 1915 Uprising

The first major act of organized anti-colonial resistance came from **John Chilembwe**, a Baptist minister educated partly in the United States. Angered by the forced conscription of Africans to fight in World War I and by the brutal conditions on settler estates, Chilembwe led an armed uprising on **January 23, 1915**, attacking the Bruce Estates plantation at Magomero and killing the estate manager. The uprising was quickly suppressed; Chilembwe was killed on **February 3, 1915**.

Though the rebellion failed militarily, Chilembwe became Malawi's first national hero and a symbol of resistance to colonial authority. His face appears on Malawian currency today.

### The Federation and the Road to Independence

In 1953, Britain imposed the **Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland**, amalgamating Nyasaland with Southern and Northern Rhodesia (present-day Zimbabwe and Zambia) into a single political unit. Malawians broadly opposed the federation, correctly perceiving it as a vehicle for white minority rule modeled on South Africa.

**Hastings Kamuzu Banda**, a physician who had spent decades practicing medicine in England and Ghana, returned to Nyasaland in 1958 and took leadership of the **Nyasaland African Congress**. In 1959, the colonial government declared a state of emergency and detained Banda and hundreds of activists. The crackdown backfired — it only accelerated the push for independence.

The federation was dissolved on **December 31, 1963**. Nyasaland achieved internal self-government the same year.

## Part III: Independence and Modern Malawi (1964–present)

### Independence

**On July 6, 1964**, Nyasaland became the independent nation of **Malawi**, with Hastings Kamuzu Banda as Prime Minister. Two years later, in 1966, Malawi was declared a republic. Banda consolidated power rapidly, banning opposition parties and establishing the **Malawi Congress Party** as the sole legal political organization. In 1971, he was declared **President-for-Life**.

### Banda's Authoritarian Rule

Banda's 30-year rule was characterized by tight ideological control, censorship, and the persecution of perceived opponents. Estimates of those killed under his regime reach approximately 18,000. Political prisoners, exiles, and enforced conformity to conservative social norms defined daily life. Banda maintained power through a network of party officials, the Malawi Young Pioneers paramilitary youth wing, and a feared security apparatus.

Economically, Banda promoted agricultural self-sufficiency and attracted some foreign investment, but the gains were unevenly distributed, and the suppression of dissent left no space for political or civil development.

### Democratic Transition

By the early 1990s, a combination of domestic protests, church-led opposition (most notably a **pastoral letter** from the Catholic bishops in 1992 condemning the regime), and the withdrawal of Western aid forced Banda to accept a referendum on multiparty democracy. In June 1993, Malawians voted overwhelmingly to end one-party rule.

In **May 1994**, **Bakili Muluzi** of the United Democratic Front defeated Banda in Malawi's first multiparty elections — ending 30 years of authoritarian rule. Banda was later tried for murder but acquitted due to failing health; he died in 1997.

### The Post-Banda Era

**Bakili Muluzi** (1994–2004) oversaw Malawi's democratic transition but faced criticism for corruption and failure to address the HIV/AIDS epidemic, which was by then killing hundreds of thousands of Malawians each year.

**Bingu wa Mutharika** (2004–2012), an economist, made early economic gains — most notably through subsidized fertilizer programs that ended food insecurity for several years and drew international attention. In his second term he became increasingly autocratic. He died in office on April 5, 2012.

His vice president, **Joyce Banda**, succeeded him — becoming **Malawi's first female president** and only the second female head of state in sub-Saharan Africa at the time. She sold the presidential jet and a fleet of luxury vehicles to fund famine relief, earning international recognition.

**Peter Mutharika**, an international law professor and Bingu's younger brother, won the 2014 election. His first term ended in controversy when the Constitutional Court annulled the 2019 election results due to widespread irregularities — a landmark ruling that ordered a fresh election, which **Lazarus Chakwera** won in June 2020. Chakwera, a theologian and former pastor, served until 2025, when Peter Mutharika returned to power in the most recent election cycle.

## Malawi Today

### Geography and Demographics

Malawi is a landlocked country in southeastern Africa, bordered by Zambia to the northwest, Tanzania to the northeast, and Mozambique on the east, south, and west. The country covers **118,484 km²** (about the size of Pennsylvania) and is dominated geographically by the **Great Rift Valley**, which runs north to south and contains **Lake Malawi** — the third-largest lake in Africa and ninth-largest in the world, covering roughly 25% of the country's total surface area.

The terrain ranges from the lakeshore lowlands to the **Shire Highlands** in the south and the **Northern Highlands** around Mzimba and Rumphi, where elevations exceed 2,000 meters. **Mount Mulanje** in the southeast (3,002 m) is the highest peak in south-central Africa.

**Population:** approximately **21 million** (2024 est.), making Malawi one of the most densely populated countries in sub-Saharan Africa. The population is predominantly rural — only about 18% live in urban areas.

**Capital and largest city:** Lilongwe (~1.3 million), relocated from Zomba in 1975.
**Second city:** Blantyre (~1.1 million metro), the commercial hub in the southern region.
**Other major cities:** Mzuzu (northern region hub, ~220,000); Zomba (former capital, ~120,000).

**Languages:** Chichewa (Chewa) is the national language, spoken across the central and southern regions. **Tumbuka** is the dominant language of the northern region — including Ekwendeni and Mzimba District. English is the official language of government and education. Approximately a dozen other languages are spoken by smaller communities.

**Religion:** Predominantly Christian (~87%), with a significant Muslim minority (~12%), concentrated largely in the southern and central regions among Yao communities.

**Ethnic groups:** Chewa (~34%), Lomwe (~19%), Yao (~13%), Ngoni (~10%), Tumbuka (~9%), and others (CIA World Factbook, 2018 census).

### Economy

Malawi is one of the least urbanized and most agriculture-dependent economies in the world. **Agriculture** accounts for roughly 60–65% of the workforce and 30% of GDP. **Tobacco** remains the single largest export commodity, accounting for roughly 50–60% of export earnings, though this share has been declining as the government pursues diversification. Other significant exports include **tea**, **sugar**, **coffee**, and **groundnuts**.

**GDP per capita** (PPP): approximately $1,600 (World Bank, 2023) — among the lowest globally.

The country has significant **mineral resources** including uranium (mined at Kayelekera), rare earth elements, and coal deposits, though extraction remains limited. **Lake Malawi fisheries** (especially chambo and usipa) are a major source of protein and livelihoods for lakeshore communities.

**Tourism** is a growing sector. Lake Malawi — sometimes called the "Calendar Lake" for its 365 miles of length and 52 miles of width — attracts visitors for freshwater diving, kayaking, and its remarkable cichlid biodiversity. **Liwonde National Park** (hippos, elephants, sable antelope) and **Nyika Plateau** are major wildlife destinations.

### Public Health: Key Statistics

Malawi carries a significant burden of communicable disease. The following figures are drawn from WHO, UNAIDS, and World Bank data (2022–2024):

**HIV/AIDS**
- Adult HIV prevalence: **8.9%** (ages 15–49; Malawi PHIA, 2020–21)
- Sub-Saharan Africa average: ~3.1% | Global average: ~0.7%
- Approximately 980,000–1.0 million people living with HIV in Malawi (UNAIDS/NAC, 2022)
- Antiretroviral therapy coverage has expanded substantially; approximately 88% of people living with HIV know their status, and of those, ~98% are on treatment

**Maternal Mortality**
- Maternal mortality ratio: **381 per 100,000 live births** (WHO, 2020)
- Sub-Saharan Africa average: ~486 | Global average: ~223
- Under-5 mortality rate: **42 per 1,000 live births**
- Global average: ~37 | Sub-Saharan Africa average: ~74

**Malaria**
- Malaria incidence: approximately **208 per 1,000 population** at risk (WHO/World Bank, 2022)
- Sub-Saharan Africa average: ~230 | Global average: ~57
- Malaria accounts for roughly 30% of all outpatient visits in Malawi

**Tuberculosis**
- TB incidence: approximately **146 per 100,000 population** (WHO, 2022)
- Global average: ~133 | High-burden African countries average significantly higher
- TB/HIV co-infection is a major clinical challenge

**Nutrition**
- Stunting prevalence in children under 5: **37%** (Malawi DHS, 2015–16); more recent estimates ~35%
- Sub-Saharan Africa average: ~31% | Global average: ~22%

### Climate and Recent Events

Malawi has a subtropical climate with a rainy season from November through April. The country is increasingly affected by climate variability. **Cyclone Idai** (2019) and **Cyclone Freddy** (2023) — one of the longest-lived and most energetic tropical cyclones ever recorded — caused catastrophic flooding in the southern region, with Freddy alone killing over 1,000 people in Malawi and displacing over 500,000.
