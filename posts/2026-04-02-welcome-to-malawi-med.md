There are moments in life when you meet a person and immediately know you’re in the presence of someone extraordinary. The way they speak. The passion they carry. The ease of their laughter. It’s really refreshing in a world that can feel heavy at times.

I remember feeling that when I sat across the coffee table with Dr. Glandson Chirwa, listening to him talk about his journey, his community, and his vision for what healthcare could look like in Ekwendeni, Malawi. You could feel it immediately: this was someone who wasn’t just practicing medicine, but building something bigger.

Glandson is now a practicing surgeon in rural Ekwendeni. He was born and raised in the area and worked as a mechanic for years before deciding medical training was how he could help his community most. He went back to medical school. Not an easy task anywhere, but one especially challenging in Malawi, where access and affordability are significant barriers. He overcame these challenges with the goal of serving his home and rural community. I understood that very deeply and was immediately inspired.

It’s remarkable how you can grow up thousands of miles apart, on completely different sides of the world, and still share the same sense of purpose. We both came to medicine a little later. We both felt drawn back to our home communities. We both want to work in rural settings and try to make a difference where it’s needed most. We both want to see what we can do to help battle the many forces that make living a healthy, happy life so difficult. I remember thinking: I want to learn everything I can from this person.

We also had the privilege of working with physicians like Dr. Stanley Nohlovu, whose dedication to patient care and teaching further shaped our experience in Ekwendeni. It quickly became clear that this work is built on a community of people deeply committed to caring for their own.

This is a fully student-developed, student-led experience. Students from a rural West Virginia medical school partner with physicians and healthcare teams in rural Ekwendeni, Malawi. I first came here in 2023 as a second-year medical student. Now, I return as a fourth-year, just one month away from graduating.

We are learners learning from the best—clinicians who work with limited resources and still find ways to deliver meaningful, life-changing care.

Malawi’s life expectancy is about 66–68 years, significantly below the global average. In McDowell County, West Virginia, it’s nearly identical, almost a decade lower than the U.S. national average. Malawi has made remarkable gains over the past two decades (life expectancy in 2000 was 44 years), showing what’s possible when public health systems are strengthened.

Different places. Similar challenges.

There is work to be done.

I’m honored to be returning to Malawi as one of 16 students participating in this global health rotation.

What an opportunity for us to take what we learn back to a place where we hope to make changes one day too.

## Student Leadership

This program exists because of an amazing group of student leaders:

Anna Mayo Eastman, fourth-year medical student, created this experience in 2023 after building connections between physicians in the U.S. and Malawi. What began with four students has grown into a 16-student program rooted in sustainable partnerships and meaningful impact. Anna has dedicated thousands of hours to building something to last for years to come. She is thoughtful, driven, witty, and endlessly kind. I’ve learned more from her than I can put into words.

Thomas Mosman, fourth-year medical student, was one of the original four students to travel to Malawi. He has an incredible ability to connect with people across cultures—not just through language, but through presence. After his first trip, he founded the Global Medical Education Foundation (https://www.globalmededfoundation.com) to expand access to medical education in Malawi. The foundation has already supported a number of students pursuing medical and nursing degrees. His commitment is truly inspiring. He even drives Uber during medical school and donates the proceeds. Again, I’ve learned so much from him.

Katie Germann, rising fourth-year medical student, was also part of the inaugural group. Though she is currently in Peru completing a Fulbright-Fogarty research scholarship, she remains a key leader in Malawi Med. During her 2023 rotation, she used her background in laboratory science to help restore a nonfunctioning blood chemistry lab and made a lasting impact. She is innovative, brilliant, and deeply committed.

Josh Eastman, fourth-year medical student, brings experience from global immersion in India and a passion for rural care. He developed our cultural humility and ethics curriculum and designed our website to share this story. He approaches problems like a builder—always thinking, always creating. It’s always an honor and genuinely fun to be part of his work.

Alongside this leadership team are 11 additional students—second-, third-, and fourth-years—who bring just as much curiosity, compassion, and commitment to this work. It’s been incredibly meaningful to be part of a group that is not only driven, but genuinely kind and grounded in a shared purpose.

In the coming weeks, our team will share more about this experience: how Malawi Med began, what we’re learning, the similarities between Appalachia and Malawi, and reflections on cultural humility, partnership, and growth.

It is a privilege to learn alongside our Malawian colleagues. And it is just as much a privilege to be surrounded by peers who are thoughtful, driven, and genuinely committed to doing good work in the world.

Thank you for following along with us.

We’re excited to share this journey.
