Stepping into a rural village in Malawi, I was reminded that healthcare begins long before a patient ever reaches a clinic. As a group of sixteen medical students, we joined local nursing students for an outreach experience that brought us face to face with the realities shaping health at the household level. We spoke with families about who lives in their homes, how they earn money, what they eat, and how far they travel for water, school, and care. Many households shared a single toilet across multiple homes, cooked in outdoor kitchens, and relied on limited, distant resources. These conversations made clear that health is deeply tied to environment, access, and daily living conditions.

We walked through the village to better understand what we were told, observing water sources, sanitation, and the long paths to schools and clinics. The nursing students led with intention, building trust and offering education on sanitation, nutrition, and family planning in ways that respected cultural and religious contexts. We were not just observing care, we were seeing what it means to meet people where they are and to understand health beyond symptoms.

This experience sharpened our perspective on access to care and the systems we often take for granted in the United States, even in rural areas like West Virginia. It also clarified our role moving forward. To care well for patients, we must understand the conditions they live in, not just the conditions they present with.
