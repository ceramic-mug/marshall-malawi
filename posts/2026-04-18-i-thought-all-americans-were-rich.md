“I thought all Americans were rich!” I have heard this phrase again and again throughout our time in Malawi. It is deeply interesting to me as someone who grew up poor in America to hear this, and further to be able to directly experience the differences and similarities between poverty in America and in Malawi. 

On Monday, we visited a local village with the nursing students from Ekwendeni as they gathered survey data. What surprised me about this visit was not how different, but how similar— and in some ways better— the village we visited was in comparison to many of the hollers and communities in West Virginia. I think the only significant and consistent difference was the persistence of outdoor toilets which have largely been replaced with indoor flushing toilets back home. 

So—This group of four villages had a local grocery, a bar, a couple shops, a primary school, and a medical clinic within walking distance. In Rosedale, WV where I am from— it is 45 minutes minimum by car to get to a grocery store, and nearly 30 by car to the nearest medical care! Walking anywhere aside from to a neighbor’s home just isn’t feasible. Many of the houses were small, but were permanent homes made with brick and mortar with iron sheeting for roofing. Contrast this to the ubiquitous pre-fab trailer— not built to last and some of which held together with duct tape, plastic tarps, and prayer. 


Now, this isn’t at all to say that America or even West Virginia are worse off than Malawi. There are important social, governmental, and economic factors that I can barely wrap my head around, much less explain— but the short of it is that being from a wealthy country, we  DO have much greater access to most resources. Sometimes, medicines and supplies simply aren’t available here. The currency here- Kwacha— is inflated to a degree that $1USD is MK1700. In a way, even some of the poorest Americans ARE rich when you do not consider beyond the simple currency conversion rate. In this way, as a visitor and a guest in this place I am beyond comfortable, I am living in luxury just with the cash that I worried would not be enough when I was packing my bags. What a paradoxical feeling it is, to hold this thought and this truth in mind while trying not to have a panic attack over whether I’ll be able to pay my electric bill this month!

It has been a fascinating and humbling experience all around to be here. Now more than ever though I am understanding that the world over, poverty is poverty. We live in a more wealthy country and have better access to MOST things— this is a great privilege that I think it would be deeply wrong to ignore— but the struggle of survival and of trying to make a good life with whatever you have I think is a human experience and doesn’t look all that different wherever you may be.

Vincent Coleman
Fourth Year Medical Student
Future Family Medicine Physician
From Braxton, West Virginia
